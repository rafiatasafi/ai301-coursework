# Adding Jazz to an Open-Source Drum Machine (and Verifying a Bot's Bug Claim)

## Overview

I play guitar and have always been drawn to creative audio work — I previously built Chladni, a real-time audio physics visualizer from scratch, and this term I wanted to put that same interest toward a real open-source contribution. I found DrumBeatRepo, a public drum-machine app, through my course's issue-selection process, and its maintainer's energy resonated with me enough that I wanted to contribute. My issue-scout tool (one of six Claude Code skills I built this term to automate parts of the open-source contribution process) graded the issue — DrumBeatRepo#270, a standing "add a missing genre" invitation — as a good fit. I ended up contributing a Jazz beat pattern. The PR (https://github.com/Babali42/DrumBeatRepo/pull/635) merged after a review round with the maintainer, and the beat is now live on the site.

## What happened

I started by running issue-scout against DrumBeatRepo#270, which graded it accept. After I posted my claim comment on the issue, though, re-running the scout came back reject — not because anything about the issue changed, but because its `not-already-claimed` check had no exemption for my own account, so it read my own successful claim (and the maintainer's approving reply) as a competing claim. I logged it in my scan-notes.md: "Found a second not-already-claimed bug: the check didn't exempt my own claim comment... Added an exemption for comments/assignments/PRs that are mine." That's the change I made, and re-running the scout afterward correctly came back accept.

Once I opened the PR, an automated review bot (CodeRabbit) left two comments: one flagging that my Hi-Hat track's `midiNote` field (42) didn't match the pedal-hi-hat part I'd described, and a second claiming the MIDI exporter's "step-position calculation" was wrong for my beat's timing. Rather than trusting or dismissing either claim, I checked them against the actual code. The first was a real mistake on my part — `midi-drum-type.ts` confirmed 42 is `CLOSED_HI_HAT` and 44 is `PEDAL_HI_HAT`, so I fixed it. The second turned out to be a genuine, but pre-existing, bug: `midi-exporter.service.ts` hardcodes `TICKS_PER_STEP = 32`, which only lines up with a real quarter note when a beat has exactly 4 subdivisions. My Jazz beat uses `subdivisionsPerBeat: 3`, so the exported timing compresses — but so would an existing beat in the repo, `psytrance-ternary.json`, which already ships with the same setting. Since fixing the exporter meant touching code outside my PR's data-only scope, I replied explaining the finding and asked the maintainer whether he'd want that fix in this PR or a separate one, rather than silently expanding scope or ignoring a real bug.

The moment that mattered most to me was finally hearing the beat. I'd disclosed from early on that I didn't have `sbt` installed locally, so I'd verified my JSON data structurally but never actually heard it play. When the maintainer requested changes and suggested two alternate samples, I opened a GitHub Codespace, ran the app, and listened for the first time. I ended up preferring my original samples and said so directly: "I preferred the original hi-hat and kick—the original combination has more of the jazz groove I was aiming for. With the suggested samples, I didn't feel that groove as strongly. ... Do you have another suggestion for the kick that might preserve that feel?" Rather than complying automatically, I explained my reasoning and asked a real question.

The maintainer responded that the trap kick's punch actually works well as contrast against the more acoustic metal kick, approved the PR, and merged it, writing: "I have complete the PR... Your beat should be online very soon! Thank you again!" He also opened a follow-up PR crediting me as a contributor for "music and demo." My Jazz beat is live on the site today.

## Limitations and what's next

This contribution was a feature addition — filling in a missing genre — rather than a bug fix. It felt genuinely good seeing it ship and go live, but it didn't give me the kind of debugging/problem-solving experience I'd get from fixing something that was actually broken. Next, I'd want to pick an issue that's a real bug fix or improvement, to get that different kind of practice. I'd also get the project's full build running locally on day one next time — not having `sbt` installed meant I shipped data I couldn't hear play for most of the project, and it silently blocked two separate test suites (Scala and Karma) for the same root cause, which I only pieced together gradually instead of discovering up front.
